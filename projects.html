01 — Applied ML · Full-stack

AI Career Copilot

Reads your CV and a job description, tells you exactly which skills are missing, and finds you something to watch for each one.

Source

The problem

You read a job posting, feel vaguely underqualified, and have no idea which specific gap is the one that matters. Generic career advice doesn't help, and pasting your CV into a third-party AI service to find out means handing over a document full of personal data.

What I built

  • A pipeline that ingests a CV as PDF or plain text and a job description as text, then extracts the skills in each using local transformer embeddings via @xenova/transformers.
  • Semantic comparison that buckets every skill into matched, missing, or nice-to-have — so the output is a decision, not a similarity score.
  • For each missing skill, a call to the YouTube Data API surfaces relevant tutorials, which can be saved into a learning plan persisted in the browser.
  • Next.js frontend, Express backend, Tailwind for the interface.

The call I'd defend

Running inference locally instead of against a hosted LLM API. It made the app free to run, usable offline, and — the part that actually mattered — meant a user's CV never leaves their machine. Embedding models are small enough that the accuracy trade-off was worth it for a task that is fundamentally about matching, not generation.

02 — NLP · Human-in-the-loop

AI Dataset Generator

A labelling factory for privacy-policy research: a fine-tuned mBERT proposes, a human disposes, and a clean dataset comes out the other end.

Source

The problem

Supervised research on privacy policies needs labelled data, and labelling policies by hand is slow, inconsistent between annotators, and mind-numbing. Worse, most existing datasets are English-only — which quietly excludes every policy that isn't.

What I built

  • Automated scraping and text-segmentation so raw policies arrive as clean, classifiable passages instead of walls of legal prose.
  • A GPU-accelerated fine-tuning pipeline for multilingual BERT, producing multi-label predictions with a confidence percentage per category.
  • A Vue.js annotation workspace where the model's suggestion is pre-filled and the human only has to confirm or correct it — turning labelling from authoring into reviewing.
  • Automated YAML export so finished datasets drop straight into downstream research pipelines.

What I took from it

The model was never the hard part. The leverage was in the interface: a human confirming a confident prediction is dramatically faster than a human labelling from a blank slate, and that single design decision did more for throughput than any amount of extra training would have. Choosing mBERT over an English-only model was the other one that mattered — it made non-English policies first-class instead of an afterthought.

03 — LLM systems · RAG

Personal AI Chatbot

A retrieval-augmented assistant that answers questions about my background from a grounded corpus instead of improvising.

Source

The problem

A CV tells you what someone did; it doesn't answer "has he actually shipped anything with Redis?" A chatbot could — but a chatbot that confidently invents details about my own career would be considerably worse than having no chatbot at all.

What I built

  • A RAG pipeline: a curated corpus about my background is embedded and searched by vector similarity, and only the retrieved passages are handed to the model as grounding.
  • An open-source LLM served through Hugging Face's APIs, rather than a proprietary hosted model.
  • A Node.js and Express backend hardened with Helmet and a CORS policy, so the API isn't an open relay to the model.
  • A React + TypeScript client with Tailwind, persistent message history, real-time responses, and error handling that fails visibly rather than silently.

What I took from it

Grounding beats model size. Once retrieval was doing its job, a modest open-source model gave better answers than a larger one guessing from its weights — because the failure mode changed from confidently wrong to honestly unsure. That trade is almost always the right one when the subject is facts about a real person.

04 — Research residency

Training-Free Temporal Grounding

Six months in the hessian.AI Lab Program, working on finding the right moment in a video without training a model to do it.

hessian.AI

The problem

Temporal grounding is the task of taking a sentence and locating the exact span of a video it describes. The standard approach is to train on densely annotated video, which is expensive to produce and doesn't transfer well beyond the domain it was annotated in.

What I worked on

  • Developed and evaluated training-free methods that use pretrained vision-language models directly, without fine-tuning on grounding annotations.
  • Built the evaluation setup to compare approaches consistently, and read into the surrounding literature to place the work properly.
  • Ran under the supervision of Prof. Dr. Marcus Rohrbach at TU Darmstadt, as a 9-ECTS component of the hessian.AI Lab Program.

What I took from it

Research discipline that carries directly into engineering: define the metric before you fall in love with the method, make comparisons genuinely like-for-like, and treat a negative result as information rather than a failure. It also made me permanently sceptical of benchmark numbers quoted without their evaluation protocol.

05 — Backend · Enterprise

HealthFlow

A Spring Boot microservice for clinical data, built as if a real hospital were going to depend on it.

Source

The problem

Healthcare software has an unusual combination of constraints: the read volume is high and repetitive, the data model is genuinely complicated — records, prescriptions, allergies, and the relationships between them — and a validation bug isn't a bad user experience, it's a safety incident.

What I built

  • A production-shaped Spring Boot microservice for patient management, with JWT authentication and role-based access control so clinical data is only reachable by the roles entitled to it.
  • Redis caching targeted at the high-frequency read paths, rather than sprinkled everywhere — the point was to cut latency where it was actually being spent.
  • Custom validators encoding medical safety rules, so invalid clinical combinations are rejected at the boundary instead of being stored and discovered later.
  • Complex domain modelling across medical records, prescriptions, and allergies, backed by comprehensive API tests.

What I took from it

In a safety-critical domain, validation is architecture. Pushing the rules into custom validators at the edge — rather than leaving them implicit in service code — meant the invariants were stated once, testable in isolation, and impossible to forget at a new call site.

06 — Full-stack · Product

StromCoach DE

Tells German households the cheapest hour to run the dishwasher, and exactly how much that decision saved them.

Source

The problem

Dynamic electricity tariffs in Germany reprice every hour, so running a high-consumption appliance at the wrong time quietly costs real money. The data to avoid that is public — but it arrives as a price curve, and nobody wants to read a chart to decide when to do laundry.

What I built

  • Live pricing pulled from the Energy Charts API through a Next.js server-side route acting as a CORS proxy, so the browser never has to fight the upstream API's origin policy.
  • A sliding-window search over the price curve that finds the cheapest contiguous block long enough to run the appliance before the user's deadline.
  • A savings calculator that answers the only question that matters — how much cheaper is this than my flat rate? — in euros, not percentages.
  • Customisable flat rate, appliance duration, and deadline, all persisted in localStorage so returning users skip setup.

What I took from it

The algorithm was the easy half. The product only became useful once the output stopped being "here is the price curve" and started being "start it at 14:00, you'll save €0.62" — a reminder that the last translation step, from correct answer to actionable one, is usually where the value actually lives.
